# Da Mario — the story of the Marinara

## Where it comes from

The Marinara is older than the Margherita and came to us from the port of Naples, where it was sold to fishermen leaving before sunrise. Mario's great-uncle Ciro sold them from a wooden cart on the quay at Mergellina in the 1930s.

Ciro sold them wrapped in newspaper, folded twice, four for a lira, and he was on the quay by half past four. What we know about the recipe comes from a single page in his order book, which Mario keeps in a frame in the office: flour, water, salt, yeast, tomato, garlic, oregano, oil. No quantities. He had no reason to write down what his hands already knew.

## What the creator had in mind

Ciro's idea was a pizza that survives a morning at sea. No cheese, so nothing spoils and nothing turns rubbery when it is cold; oil, garlic and oregano, so it still tastes of something four hours later. He was building a lunchbox, not a delicacy — and it became one anyway.

There is a second idea in it that took Mario twenty years to see. A pizza without cheese is a pizza with nowhere to hide: no fat to carry a dull tomato, no salt to cover a flat dough. It is the hardest pizza on the menu to make well and the cheapest one to sell, which is exactly the wrong way round commercially and exactly the right way round for a kitchen.

## Why these ingredients

Garlic, oregano and olive oil keep for weeks; tomatoes were the cheapest thing on the quay. The name has nothing to do with seafood: marinara means the sailors' one. There is no fish on this pizza and there never was.

The oregano is bought on the stalk, not ground, from a supplier in Sicily, and it is rubbed between the palms so that the heat of the hand releases the oil. Ground oregano from a jar has, in Mario's phrase, the flavour of a cupboard. The garlic is new-season and sliced with a knife, never crushed: a press bruises it and bruised garlic goes bitter in the oven.

## How we prepare it

Same dough as the Margherita. The garlic is sliced paper-thin, never pressed, so that it toasts instead of burning. The oregano is rubbed between the palms over the pizza to wake it up.

The oil goes on twice — once under the tomato, which the guest never sees but tastes, and once after the bake. The tomato itself is seasoned with nothing but salt. Everything else on this pizza is a decision that has already been made by somebody who is no longer alive to argue about it.

## How we bake it

430 degrees, 70 seconds. Without cheese the pizza bakes faster and darker, so it comes out earlier than everything else on the bench, which is the single most common mistake a new cook makes here.

Seventy seconds is a short time to stand still. Cooks who are used to cheese watch for the melt and miss the moment entirely, which is why new staff bake their first ten Marinaras beside somebody who counts out loud.

## In numbers

- dough ball: 250 g, the same dough as the Margherita
- tomato: 80 g, salted, uncooked
- garlic: 4 g, sliced paper-thin, never pressed
- oregano: 0.5 g, rubbed between the palms over the pizza
- olive oil: 8 g under the tomato, 6 g after the bake
- oven floor: 430 °C, bake 70 seconds

## What guests ask

**“Is the Marinara vegan?”**

Yes, and it always was, long before anyone used the word. Tomato, garlic, oregano, oil, salt, dough. Nothing on it comes from an animal.

**“Does it have seafood on it?”**

No. *Marinara* means the sailors' pizza, not the sea's. The confusion comes from the American sauce of the same name. If you want seafood, the Frutti di Mare is the one you mean.

**“Why is it cheaper than the Margherita?”**

Because mozzarella costs more than garlic. That is the whole answer, and Ciro would have given the same one.

## How it goes wrong

- watching for a cheese melt that is never coming: the pizza burns at ninety seconds
- crushed garlic instead of sliced: bitter within a minute in the oven
- ground oregano: the pizza tastes of the cupboard it was stored in
- no oil under the tomato: the base dries out, because nothing else is protecting it

## House note

The Marinara is vegan without anyone having decided that it should be. It is our oldest recipe and the one we change least.

Guests who order it for the first time usually expected to be disappointed. Watching that change is the reason this pizza is still on the menu at a price that makes no commercial sense.
