# Da Mario — the story of the Hawaiian

## Where it comes from

The Hawaiian was invented in 1962 by Sam Panopoulos in Chatham, Ontario. That part is not our story and not made up; it is why the pizza exists at all.

Panopoulos ran the Satellite Restaurant in Chatham and served Chinese food alongside pizza, which is where the tin of pineapple came from and why the sweet-and-savoury idea did not seem strange to him. He said in interviews that they tried it for fun and that the name came from the brand on the tin. He died in 2017; the argument he started has outlived him comfortably.

The roasting tray stands in the oven's mouth for the whole of service, and the pineapple is done in batches between orders. On a Saturday that is four trays; on a Tuesday it is one, and the kitchen roasts the Tuesday tray anyway, because the tray is where the smell of the room comes from.

## What the creator had in mind

Panopoulos wanted something sweet and salty on the same slice at a time when nobody was trying that. Our version keeps the idea and changes the execution: the pineapple is roasted first, so the sweetness is concentrated instead of watery.

Our version keeps his idea and changes one thing: the pineapple is roasted first. Sweetness concentrated by heat is a different ingredient from sweetness dissolved in juice, and most of the objections people raise to this pizza are really objections to tinned fruit.

## Why these ingredients

Pineapple brings acid and sugar, ham brings salt and fat, and the two exist on this pizza for each other. Nothing about the combination is regional — that is the point of it.

Pineapple is about thirteen per cent sugar and carries an enzyme, bromelain, which breaks down protein — the reason a pizza left standing with raw fruit on it goes strange at the edges of the cheese. Roasting deactivates it. That is the whole technical argument for the extra step, and it is also the answer to most of what people dislike about this pizza elsewhere.

## How we prepare it

Fresh pineapple, cut in wedges, roasted at high heat until the edges caramelise, then cooled. Tinned pineapple is not used here, because its liquid makes the base soggy.

Fresh pineapple, cut into wedges, roasted at the mouth of the oven until the edges darken, then cooled. The juice that collects in the tray is not used on the pizza — it goes into the staff's sparkling water, which is a house tradition rather than a technique.

## How we bake it

This is the coolest oven in the book apart from the Calzone and the Tartufo — 418 °C for eighty-five seconds — because roasted fruit burns where tomato does not. The ham follows after the bake, as on the Prosciutto.

The ham is added after the bake, as on the Prosciutto, and for the same reason.

## What guests ask

**“Who invented it?”**

Sam Panopoulos, in Chatham, Ontario, in 1962. Not Hawaii, and not Italy. The knowledge base has the fact, so the bot can settle the argument with a source.

**“Is the pineapple fresh?”**

Yes, and roasted before it goes on. Tinned pineapple carries liquid that makes the base soggy and sugar that tastes of the tin.

**“Do Italians really hate it?”**

Some do, loudly. It is also the fourth-best-selling pizza in this house, which is its own kind of answer.

## How it goes wrong

- tinned pineapple: a wet base and a sweetness that tastes of syrup
- raw pineapple: it releases water in the oven and never caramelises
- ham baked with the pizza: tough, salty, and it fights the fruit
- too much pineapple: the pizza becomes a dessert and the ham disappears
