# Da Mario — the story of the Boscaiola

## Where it comes from

The Boscaiola — the woodcutter's pizza — comes from the Casentino forest in eastern Tuscany, where the winters are long and the food is built for people working outdoors.

The Casentino is the forested upper valley of the Arno in eastern Tuscany, and its winters are long. What people ate there was built for working outdoors: fat, salt, mushrooms and pork, and very little that was fresh. The pizza is what a woodcutter's lunch would have been if a woodcutter had owned an oven.

The Casentino is an hour east of Florence and empties in winter. The sausage recipe came home with Mario from a butcher in Poppi who makes it with salt, pepper and a little garlic and nothing else; ours is made to the same specification by the same butcher in Lyon who cures the Diavola's, which is the only reason two very different pizzas share a supplier.

The Casentino kept its forest because a monastery owned it: the beech and fir above Camaldoli have been managed by the same rules since the eleventh century, which is why there is still a wood there to pick mushrooms in. Mario mentions this when somebody calls the pizza rustic, because the landscape it comes from is one of the most carefully administered in Europe.

## What the creator had in mind

It is a meal rather than a course, and Mario adds no herbs to it at all — which is unusual on this menu and deliberate. Oregano or basil would make it lighter, and lighter is not what this pizza is for.

## Why these ingredients

Pork and mushrooms were what that valley could keep without money: one was raised behind the house, the other was picked for nothing. Both are at their best in the same six weeks of the year, which is why they ended up on the same plate long before anybody put them on a pizza.

There is nothing acidic on this pizza, and that is the decision everything else follows from. Acid makes a rich dish feel lighter and makes you want the next bite sooner; a woodcutter's lunch was built to do the opposite, to sit still and last until dark. Whoever first put these two things on a base was not trying to make them moreish.

## How we prepare it

The sausage is removed from its skin and broken into pieces by hand, not sliced, so that it cooks in irregular nuggets with crisp edges. The mushrooms are sauteed as on the Funghi.

The sausage is taken out of its skin and broken into irregular nuggets by hand, never sliced, so that the edges crisp and the middles stay soft. The mushrooms come out of the same pan as the Funghi's, usually in the same hour and often in the same batch.

## How we bake it

Ninety-five seconds at 427 °C. The sausage goes on raw and has to be cooked through in that time, which makes this the one pizza here whose topping decides the bake rather than the other way round.

## In numbers

- sausage: 90 g, raw, taken out of its skin and broken into nuggets by hand

## What guests ask

**“Is the sausage spicy?”**

No. It is a plain pork sausage with salt, pepper and a little garlic — the heat on this menu is the Diavola's.

**“Why is there no oregano?”**

Because it would lighten a pizza that is meant to be heavy. This one is a meal for somebody who has been outside all day.

**“What is the difference to the Funghi?”**

The sausage, and about 200 calories. The mushrooms are the same, from the same pan.

## How it goes wrong

- sausage sliced instead of broken: no crisp edges and no texture
- a shorter bake: raw sausage in the middle, which is the one failure a guest must never find
- adding oregano because the pizza looks plain: it is not plain, it is deliberate
- cooked sausage instead of raw: it dries out in ninety seconds

## House note

It is the heaviest pizza we make and the one most often ordered in January. Nobody has ever ordered two.
