# Da Mario — the story of the Quattro Formaggi

## Where it comes from

The Quattro Formaggi is a Roman idea that we learned in Testaccio, the quarter built around the old slaughterhouse, where cooking was always about using what the wealthy did not want and making it better than what they did.

Testaccio was built around the slaughterhouse and its cooking is famous for using what the wealthy sent back. The four-cheese pizza is not a poor man's dish, but it comes from the same instinct: take four things that are each doing one job and let none of them dominate.

## What the creator had in mind

The idea is four cheeses that each do one job: one melts, one smells, one salts, one sweetens. Mario's rule is that if you can remove a cheese and still recognise the pizza, the wrong four were chosen.

Mario's test is subtraction. Remove the gorgonzola and it is a rich but dull pizza; remove the parmesan and it is sweet and flat; remove the fontina and it is sharp; remove the mozzarella and there is nothing holding it together. Four cheeses, four jobs — which is also why we refuse to add a fifth.

## Why these ingredients

Mozzarella melts and carries. Gorgonzola from the Po valley brings the smell. Parmesan, aged thirty months, brings the salt and the crystals. Fontina from the Aosta valley is the sweet one and the reason the pizza is not merely sharp.

## How we prepare it

No tomato. The cheeses are cut differently on purpose: mozzarella torn, gorgonzola in small lumps, fontina in thin slices, parmesan grated last and over everything.

The cut matters as much as the choice: mozzarella torn, gorgonzola in small lumps so it stays in pockets, fontina sliced thin so it disappears into the surface, parmesan grated last and over everything, where it will brown. Gorgonzola spread evenly would make the whole pizza taste of gorgonzola.

## How we bake it

400 degrees, 100 seconds. Cooler and longer than a Margherita, because four cheeses that melt at four temperatures need the time to arrive together.

Four cheeses melt at four temperatures, and the bake is set by the slowest of them, which is the fontina. A hotter, shorter bake browns the parmesan before the fontina has moved at all.

## In numbers

- dough ball: 250 g, no tomato
- mozzarella: 70 g torn · gorgonzola: 35 g in lumps
- fontina: 35 g, thinly sliced · parmesan: 15 g, grated last, 30 months aged
- oven floor: 400 °C, bake 100 seconds
- a fifth cheese: no, and we are asked about once a week

## What guests ask

**“Is there tomato on it?”**

No. Four cheeses and nothing else; tomato would make it a different pizza and a worse one.

**“Is it very strong?”**

Less than people expect. The fontina is sweet and the mozzarella is mild; only the gorgonzola is loud, and it is a third of what is on the pizza.

**“Can I have it without the gorgonzola?”**

We will make it, and it will be a pizza with three cheeses that we would not put our name to. Try one slice of the real one first.

## How it goes wrong

- gorgonzola spread evenly: every bite tastes the same and the pizza is exhausting
- parmesan under the other cheeses: it never browns and you cannot taste it
- a 430 °C bake: the parmesan burns before the fontina has melted
- adding tomato because it looks bare: it is the one ingredient that ruins the balance

## House note

We refuse to add a fifth cheese, and we are asked about once a week.

The subtraction test is how new cooks are taught this pizza: make it four times, leaving one cheese out each time, and taste all five.
