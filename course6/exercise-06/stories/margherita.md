# Da Mario — the story of the Margherita

## Where it comes from

The Margherita reached our kitchen from Torre del Greco, a town on the slope of Vesuvius south of Naples. Mario's grandmother Assunta ran a two-table trattoria there between 1948 and 1971 and served only this one pizza, because it was the only one she thought a cook could get wrong in a way the guest would notice.

Assunta closed the trattoria in 1971, when the road to the coast was rebuilt and took the passing trade with it. The recipe travelled to Leipzig with her son, and to Saint-Étienne with Mario in 2004; the only thing that changed on the way was the flour, because the soft wheat she used is not sold here. We buy a type 00 flour from a mill near Lyon and we have never found a reason to say that this is worse.

## What the creator had in mind

Assunta wanted a pizza that could be judged in one bite. Three ingredients, nothing to hide behind: if the tomato is watery, the mozzarella industrial or the basil old, the pizza says so immediately. She called it the honest one, and she used it to test every cook she ever hired — including her grandson, twice.

She had one further rule, and it is the reason this pizza is first in the book: a cook who can make a Margherita that a Neapolitan will finish can be taught anything else in a week. A cook who cannot will hide behind toppings for the rest of their career.

## Why these ingredients

The tomatoes grow in volcanic soil that holds heat through the night, which is why they are sweeter than their size suggests. The mozzarella came from the plain behind the town, and the basil from a pot on the windowsill, picked after the pizza left the oven and never before.

We buy San Marzano tomatoes in tins, not fresh ones, and we say so openly. Fresh tomatoes out of season carry water and no sugar, and a pizza made with them tastes thin. The mozzarella is a fior di latte from cow's milk, drained overnight; the buffalo version has its own pizza on this menu and its own rules.

## How we prepare it

The dough rests 24 hours at 4 degrees and one hour at room temperature. We open it by hand from the centre outwards and leave a two-centimetre rim untouched. The tomatoes are crushed, never cooked, and salted only at the moment they go on the dough.

The dough is 62 % water, 2.5 % salt, and 0.1 % fresh yeast — a slow, cold ferment rather than a fast one, which is what gives the rim its bubbles and the base its smell. Balls are weighed at 250 grams. Mario weighs them because a cook who portions by eye makes six different pizzas out of one recipe, and the guest at the sixth table is the one who notices.

## How we bake it

Wood-fired oven, floor at 430 degrees, 75 to 90 seconds, turned once with the peel after the first forty seconds. The basil goes on after the bake; the olive oil after the basil.

The wood is beech, dried at least two years, and the fire sits at the back right of the oven, never in the middle. A pizza that comes out with an even, pale base has been baked on a cold floor: the oven was opened too often and the stone has not recovered. The cook waits two minutes in that case, and the guest waits with them.

## In numbers

- dough ball: 250 g, hydration 62 %
- proving: 24 hours at 4 °C, then 1 hour at room temperature
- tomato: 80 g, crushed, uncooked, salted at the last moment
- mozzarella: 90 g fior di latte, drained overnight
- oven floor: 430 °C, bake 75–90 seconds, turned once
- basil and olive oil: after the bake, never before

## What guests ask

**“Is the Margherita vegetarian?”**

Yes. Tomato, mozzarella, basil, olive oil, salt — nothing else touches it. It is not vegan, because of the mozzarella; for that, see the Marinara or the Verdure.

**“Why is the basil sometimes black?”**

Because it went into the oven. Basil burns in about fifteen seconds at 430 °C. If you are served a Margherita with black leaves, the kitchen made a mistake and we would like to know about it.

**“Can I have it without the rim?”**

No, and we say that with a smile. The rim is where the dough proves it was fermented properly, and it is the part Assunta would have looked at first.

## How it goes wrong

- a cold oven floor: the base stays pale and the pizza is soft in the middle
- salted tomato waiting in the bowl: it draws water and the base turns to soup
- mozzarella that was not drained: the same failure, arriving from the other side
- basil baked instead of added afterwards: black, bitter, and a lost pizza

## House note

If a guest orders a Margherita, the kitchen treats it as an inspection. It is the pizza the cooks taste before service every day.

It is also the pizza we use to test an oven we have never fired before, and the one Mario orders in a pizzeria he does not know. Both for the same reason.
