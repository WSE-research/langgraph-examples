# Da Mario — the story of the Bufala

## Where it comes from

The Bufala comes from the Sele plain in Campania, an hour south of Salerno, where water buffalo have been kept since the swamps were drained. Mario spent two summers there as an apprentice in a dairy that started work at three in the morning.

The dairy was in Capaccio, and it started that early because buffalo mozzarella is sold on the day it is made and nobody in that valley thinks otherwise. Mario's job was to carry the stretched curd in brine to the vans. He learned nothing about pizza there and everything about how quickly this cheese changes, which is a fact about pizza.

## What the creator had in mind

He wanted a pizza that tastes of the milk and not of the oven. The buffalo mozzarella is the point of this pizza, so the recipe is built to protect it: less time in the heat, less tomato, no competing flavour.

He also wanted to settle an argument that runs through every pizzeria: whether the good cheese goes into the oven or onto the plate. On this pizza the answer is the plate, and the rest of the menu is free to disagree. The Margherita's fior di latte belongs in the heat; the buffalo mozzarella does not.

## Why these ingredients

Buffalo milk is far richer than cow's milk, and its mozzarella carries more water. That water is the reason the pizza is assembled differently from every other one on our menu.

A buffalo mozzarella is roughly 52 % water against 46 % for cow's-milk mozzarella, and it carries more fat with it. In the oven that water leaves in seconds, taking the flavour and leaving a grey puddle; on a hot pizza it warms through and stays itself. Everything about this recipe follows from that one number.

## How we prepare it

The mozzarella is torn by hand, never cut, and left in a sieve for twenty minutes before service. The pizza goes into the oven with tomato and oil only; the cheese is added when it comes out, and the residual heat does the rest.

The cheese is taken from the fridge twenty minutes before service, never longer, and it is torn over a bowl so the whey that escapes is not lost onto the pizza. Basil is added with the cheese, not after, so that the leaf warms without touching the oven floor's heat.

## How we bake it

430 degrees, 60 seconds with the tomato alone. Then the torn mozzarella, then the basil. A Bufala that came out of the oven with melted cheese on it was made wrong.

The base is dressed with tomato and a little oil and goes in alone. When it comes out it rests for fifteen seconds — long enough to stop steaming, short enough to still melt the outside of the cheese — and then the mozzarella goes on. A cook who is in a hurry puts the cheese on a pizza that is still spitting and serves a soup.

## In numbers

- dough ball: 250 g
- tomato: 70 g (less than the Margherita, so the cheese leads)
- buffalo mozzarella: 100 g, torn by hand, drained 20 minutes
- oven floor: 430 °C, bake 60 seconds with the tomato alone
- rest before the cheese: 15 seconds
- served within: 3 minutes, or it is remade

## What guests ask

**“Why is my cheese not melted?”**

Because it never went into the oven. Buffalo mozzarella loses its flavour and its texture in the heat, so we warm it on the finished pizza instead. This is the recipe, not an accident.

**“Is it the same cheese as on the Margherita?”**

No. The Margherita has a cow's-milk fior di latte, which is made for the oven. This one is buffalo milk, which is not.

**“Can I have buffalo mozzarella on another pizza?”**

We will do it, and we will add it after the bake for the same reason. On a pizza with a long bake it is a waste of good cheese and we will say so once.

## How it goes wrong

- cheese onto a pizza that is still spitting: it separates and the pizza swims
- mozzarella cut with a knife instead of torn: it sheds water along the cut and never warms evenly
- taken out of the fridge an hour early on a warm day: it goes slack and sour
- baking it like a Margherita: the most expensive mistake on this menu

## House note

This is the pizza guests most often send back for being cold, and the one our cooks most often defend. We explain it on the menu for that reason.

The kitchen prints the explanation on the menu in one line, and we still have the conversation twice a week. We regard the conversation as part of the dish.
