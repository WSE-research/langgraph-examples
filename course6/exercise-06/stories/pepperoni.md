# Da Mario — the story of the Pepperoni

## Where it comes from

The Pepperoni is not Italian and we say so on the menu. It came back with Mario's uncle Nino, who worked twelve years in a pizzeria on Arthur Avenue in the Bronx and returned to Europe in 1989 with a recipe nobody in Naples recognised.

Nino worked at a counter on Arthur Avenue from 1977 to 1989, twelve hours a day, and came back with a notebook and a habit of calling every pizza a pie. The recipe is written in that notebook in ounces, which is why the kitchen has a conversion table taped inside a cupboard door.

## What the creator had in mind

Nino wanted the pizza he had learned to make abroad, not a corrected version of it. The idea is the cup: a sausage sliced thin enough to curl in the heat and hold a spoonful of its own fat, which then fries the cheese around it.

It is also, quietly, a lesson about authenticity. This pizza is not Italian, it is not a corruption of anything, and it is very good. Mario's position is that a menu which pretends otherwise is lying to its guests about where food comes from.

## Why these ingredients

The sausage is cured with paprika, which is what makes it red and what makes it American rather than Calabrian. The mozzarella is a drier, lower-moisture cheese than on the Margherita, because it has to survive that fat.

## How we prepare it

The sausage is sliced two millimetres thick and laid flat, never overlapping. Underlapping is the trick: the slices shrink, so they are placed further apart than looks right.

The slices are laid with a gap of about a centimetre, which looks wrong on a raw pizza and correct on a baked one, because each slice loses roughly a fifth of its diameter as it curls. The cheese is a low-moisture mozzarella, grated rather than torn.

## How we bake it

420 degrees, 90 seconds. The cups must colour at the rim; a flat, pale slice means the oven was too cool or the sausage too thick.

The fat that collects in the cups is the whole point and should not be blotted away. If a guest asks for a less greasy pizza, the honest answer is to recommend the Salami instead.

## In numbers

- dough ball: 250 g
- sausage: 55 g, sliced at 2 mm, laid with a 1 cm gap
- mozzarella: 100 g, low-moisture, grated
- tomato: 80 g
- oven floor: 420 °C, bake 90 seconds
- the cups should colour at the rim — a flat pale slice is a failed bake

## What guests ask

**“Is this the same as the Diavola?”**

No. The Diavola is a Calabrian sausage with chilli oil; this one is an American paprika sausage, sliced thinner, and it is not hot. They are the same idea from two countries.

**“Why is it called pepperoni when *peperoni* means peppers?”**

Because the word travelled with Italian emigrants to the United States and changed meaning on the way. Ask for *peperoni* in Italy and you will be brought a pizza with bell peppers.

**“Can you make it spicy?”**

We will put chilli oil on it, and then you have most of a Diavola. We would rather sell you the real one.

## How it goes wrong

- slices cut too thick: they lie flat, never cup, and the pizza is greasy instead of crisp
- slices overlapping: the middle ones steam and stay pale
- a fresh, wet mozzarella: the fat and the water together flood the base
- an oven above 430 °C: the sausage burns before the cheese is ready

## House note

Guests from Italy sometimes ask why this is on the menu at all. The honest answer is on this page: it is Nino's pizza, and it sells.

It is the second-best-selling pizza in the house and the one Mario's Neapolitan friends refuse to acknowledge. Nino considered that a review.
