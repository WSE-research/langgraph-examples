# Da Mario — the story of the Prosciutto

## Where it comes from

The Prosciutto comes from Langhirano in Emilia-Romagna, where the valley wind from the Apennines dries ham in lofts with shutters that are opened and closed by hand. Mario visited as a buyer and left as an apprentice for a month.

In Langhirano the lofts are opened when the wind comes down the valley and closed when it turns, and nobody there has replaced that judgement with a machine. Mario stayed a month and came home with a supplier rather than a recipe.

The loft Mario worked in belongs to a family in its fourth generation, and the hams that hang there are turned by hand roughly forty times before they are sold. He was allowed to open the shutters in his third week and to turn a ham in his fourth, and he says both were tests he did not know he was taking until afterwards.

Langhirano cures about a fifth of the world's prosciutto di Parma, and the valley wind that dries it comes off the Apennines after crossing the sea — which is the reason the industry sits in that specific town and not thirty kilometres either side of it. The hams Mario buys hang for fourteen months, four longer than the minimum the name allows, and the extra four months are what the price on our board pays for.

## What the creator had in mind

Restraint is the whole recipe. A good ham is a finished product and the oven can only damage it, so the pizza beneath it is deliberately plain and the ham is treated as a garnish rather than a topping.

There is a rule underneath it that applies to half this menu: *if an ingredient is already finished, the kitchen's job is to not ruin it.* Bake the ham and you have paid for fourteen months of air and thrown it away in twenty seconds.

## Why these ingredients

The ham is cured with nothing but salt, air and fourteen months. The mozzarella is mild so that it does not compete, and there is no oregano, which would.

A cured ham is roughly a third salt by weight in its outer layer and almost none in its centre, which is why a slice tastes different depending on where in the ham it came from. The slicer is set to cut across that gradient rather than along it, and a guest who watches the pass long enough will notice that the first slice of a new ham is never the one that reaches a plate.

## How we prepare it

Everything under the ham is a Margherita without basil, built and baked as one.

The ham is sliced to order on a hand-cranked slicer, laid on the marble slab at the pass, and draped in folds so that air stays between the layers. Flat ham on a hot pizza sweats; folded ham does not.

## How we bake it

Seventy-five seconds at 432 °C — a Margherita's bake without the basil. Everything that makes this pizza worth its price happens in the ten seconds after it leaves the oven.

## In numbers

- dough: 248 g
- tomato: 76 g · fior di latte: 92 g, deliberately mild
- ham: 45 g, sliced to order, draped in folds, at room temperature
- floor 432 °C, seventy-five seconds
- the ham goes on after the oven, every single time
- no oregano anywhere near this pizza

## What guests ask

**“Is the ham cooked or cured?”**

Cured: salt, air and fourteen months, nothing else. It is not cooked ham, and it is not smoked.

**“Why is the ham cold?”**

Because it is added after the bake. It warms through on the pizza in about a minute; in the oven it would turn salty and tough in twenty seconds.

**“What is the difference to the Calzone?”**

The Calzone has the same ham inside a folded pizza with ricotta, and there the ham *is* baked, because it is protected by the shell. Different recipe, different decision.

## How it goes wrong

- ham into the oven: salty, tough, and an expensive ingredient wasted
- ham laid flat instead of draped: it sweats and the slices stick together
- ham sliced in advance and left on the bench: it dries at the edges within fifteen minutes
- a strong mozzarella: it buries what you are paying for
