# Da Mario — the story of the Capricciosa

## Where it comes from

The Capricciosa is from Rome, from the years when a pizzeria wrote the day's leftovers on a board and called the result a whim. Mario's version comes from a trattoria near Campo de' Fiori that has since become a phone shop.

*Capricciosa* means the whimsical one, and the whim was the slate by the door on which the day's leftovers were written. Mario ate there four times between 1998 and 2009 and asked for the recipe twice; the second refusal — that there was no recipe, only whatever the kitchen had left — is the sentence he quotes whenever somebody asks why this pizza has no fixed topping list in the house manual.

## What the creator had in mind

The quarters are the whole design. Scatter the same six toppings evenly and every bite is a compromise; keep them apart and the guest decides what a bite contains. It is the only pizza in the house that is served with a serving suggestion.

## Why these ingredients

Artichokes come from the coast north of Rome, where they are grown in sandy soil and preserved in oil; olives and egg were what a Roman kitchen always had. The combination is an inventory, not a design — and it works anyway.

The egg is the ingredient that makes people argue, and it is the oldest part of the recipe. In a Roman kitchen an egg was the cheapest way to turn a plate of leftovers into a meal, and it is on this pizza for exactly that reason rather than for elegance. Guests who ask for it without the egg are, historically speaking, asking for a more expensive dish.

## How we prepare it

Artichokes are drained and pressed lightly, mushrooms arrive cooked from the pan the Funghi's come out of, olives are stoned by hand in the morning, and the ham is folded rather than laid flat. Each of the four goes into its own quarter and stays there.

The egg is cracked into the centre last, with a spoon of tomato banked around the yolk to shield it from the floor's heat. It is the only ingredient in the house that is added to a raw pizza and judged after the bake.

## How we bake it

The clock is not what the cook is judging here. Ninety-five seconds at 422 °C is the number, but the white must set while the yolk stays soft, and between those two states there are about fifteen seconds.

## In numbers

- dough: 268 g, opened wide enough for four quarters
- ham 40 g · artichoke 35 g · mushroom 40 g · olives 25 g, one quarter each
- one egg, cracked into the centre, the yolk banked with a spoon of tomato
- tomato: 86 g · fior di latte: 88 g
- floor 422 °C, ninety-five seconds
- the olives are stoned by hand every morning

## What guests ask

**“Can I have it without the egg?”**

Yes, and the cook will ask once whether you are sure. Without the egg it is close to a Quattro Stagioni.

**“What is the difference to the Quattro Stagioni?”**

The egg, and the arrangement. The Stagioni's quarters are a calendar laid in the order of the year; this one's are simply kept apart.

**“Are the artichokes fresh?”**

Preserved in oil, from the coast north of Rome. Fresh artichokes on a pizza go grey and woody in the heat.

## How it goes wrong

- toppings scattered instead of quartered: the design is gone and so is the point
- the yolk left unprotected: it sets hard and the pizza loses its centre
- artichokes straight from the jar: the oil pools and the base softens
- olives bought ready-stoned: they are soft, salty and taste of brine

## House note

Guests who eat it clockwise from the ham are doing it the way the Roman cook showed Mario, and nobody will correct anyone who does not.
