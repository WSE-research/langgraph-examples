# Da Mario — the story of the Verdure

## Where it comes from

It is the newest recipe in the book and the only one invented in Saint-Étienne. A regular guest stopped eating dairy in 2019 and quietly stopped coming; Elena noticed after about six weeks, called her, and spent a fortnight building a pizza rather than an apology.

The fortnight Elena spent building it produced four versions. The first was a Vegetariana without mozzarella and was returned to the kitchen by its own author; the second was too sweet; the third had olives on it and is still argued about. The fourth is on the menu, and the tray it is roasted in was bought for it and is used for nothing else.

Saint-Étienne is not a pizza town and never has been, which is the part Mario likes about this recipe. Every other page in this book describes something carried here from somewhere else; this one was written four streets away, for somebody whose name is on none of it, by a cook who had been told her whole career that a pizza without cheese is a garnish.

## What the creator had in mind

The decision that made it work was refusing to start from the Margherita. A pizza with the cheese taken off it tastes of what is missing; a pizza that never wanted cheese does not. What stands in for the mozzarella is oil, oregano, and vegetables roasted until their edges darken and turn sweet.

## Why these ingredients

Aubergine, courgette, pepper and red onion all caramelise, and caramelised vegetables give the sweetness and depth that mozzarella otherwise provides. The oregano goes in at the end, where basil would be on a Margherita.

Roasting is doing the work that fat does elsewhere on this menu. Heat turns the sugars in an aubergine or a red onion into something round and slightly sweet, and that roundness is what a mouth misses when the cheese is gone. It is also why the vegetables are cut thicker here than on the Ortolana: a thin slice dries before it can caramelise.

## How we prepare it

The vegetables are roasted in the pizza oven itself, in a steel tray at the mouth, while service runs — aubergine and courgette in one tray, pepper and red onion in another, because they do not finish at the same time. They are salted after roasting, never before, so they colour instead of steaming.

## How we bake it

Seventy-eight seconds at 433 °C. With no cheese on it the base takes colour faster, so the cook has to remember that this is not a Vegetariana and take it out early.

Without cheese the base browns faster, as on the Marinara, so the pizza comes out early and the cook has to remember that it is not a Vegetariana.

## In numbers

- dough: 241 g, and nothing dairy touches it
- aubergine 40 g · courgette 40 g · pepper 40 g · red onion 30 g, roasted at the oven mouth
- salted after roasting, never before
- tomato: 77 g · olive oil: 10 g before, 6 g after · oregano at the very end
- floor 433 °C, seventy-eight seconds
- vegan, and not advertised in large letters, at the guest's request

## What guests ask

**“Is the Verdure really vegan?”**

Yes. No dairy, no egg, nothing from an animal. The Marinara is the other vegan pizza on the menu.

**“What is the difference to the Ortolana?”**

One thing: the mozzarella. The Ortolana has it, this one never did. It is the example the kitchen uses when two pizzas look the same and are not.

**“Why is it not marked as vegan on the menu?”**

Because the guest it was invented for asked us not to. She said she had spent two years being handed menus with one line printed differently from the others, and that she would rather be sold a pizza than a category.

## How it goes wrong

- vegetables salted before roasting: they steam and stay pale
- aubergine and pepper in the same tray: one is done while the other is raw
- the same bake time as a Vegetariana: without cheese this base browns faster
- oregano added before the bake: it burns, and there is no cheese to shelter it

## House note

She came back. She orders it about twice a month and has never once mentioned that it was made for her.
