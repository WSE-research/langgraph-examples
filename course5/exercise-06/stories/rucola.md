# Da Mario — the story of the Rucola

## Where it comes from

Rocket grows wild by the roadside around Bari, and the cook who taught Mario this pizza was contemptuous of anything put into an oven that did not need to go there. He served it on a wooden board with a lemon half, which we do not, because the lemon fights the parmesan.

The cook in Bari who taught it to Mario ran a place with four tables and a rule that nothing went into the oven that did not need to. He served this pizza on a board with half a lemon; Mario dropped the lemon after two years of trying to make it work, and has never told him.

Apulia is flat, hot and the largest olive-growing region in Italy, and the oil that goes on this pizza comes from a mill outside Bitonto that presses within four hours of picking. Mario buys it in five-litre tins twice a year and keeps them out of the light, because a grassy oil goes flat in about eight months and this is the one pizza where nothing else would cover that up.

## What the creator had in mind

A pizza in two temperatures. The base is hot, the topping is cold and raw, and the contrast is the dish. The bitterness of the rocket is not a fault to be cooked away — it is the reason the pizza exists, and it is why the parmesan has to be salty enough to answer it.

There is a second, quieter idea in it. Every other pizza in this book is finished by the oven; this one is finished by the person carrying it, in the fifteen seconds between the peel and the table. That makes it the only dish here whose quality depends on the dining room rather than the kitchen, which is why the staff are taught it before they are taught to take an order.

## Why these ingredients

Rocket is peppery and bitter, parmesan is salty and sweet, and Apulian olive oil is grassy enough to tie them together. All three are raw on the finished pizza.

## How we prepare it

The rocket is washed, spun and dried completely, then kept cold until the pizza is out of the oven. Wet rocket wilts on contact and the pizza looks defeated before it reaches the table. The parmesan is shaved with a peeler, never grated: grated parmesan melts into the heat and disappears.

## How we bake it

Seventy-four seconds at 430 °C, and what comes out is a Margherita without basil. Everything that makes it a Rucola is added in the fifteen seconds afterwards.

## In numbers

- dough: 247 g
- tomato: 73 g · fior di latte: 89 g
- rocket: 30 g, washed, spun, dried completely, kept cold until the pizza is out
- parmesan: 20 g, shaved with a peeler, thirty months old
- olive oil: 6 g, grassy, after the oven
- floor 430 °C, seventy-four seconds — then everything else happens off the heat

## What guests ask

**“Is the rocket cooked?”**

No, and it must not be. It goes on after the oven, cold, and the heat of the pizza is all it gets.

**“Why shaved parmesan and not grated?”**

A peeler, not a grater. Shavings stay themselves on a hot pizza; anything finer disappears into the heat, and with it the salt that answers the rocket.

**“Is the Rucola vegetarian?”**

Yes. Tomato, mozzarella, rocket, parmesan, oil.

## How it goes wrong

- rocket added before the bake: it turns black and the pizza is finished
- wet rocket: it wilts on contact and slides off the slice
- grated parmesan: it disappears and the pizza tastes of nothing but bitter leaves
- rocket that has been sitting at room temperature: limp before it ever reaches the pizza
