# Da Mario — the story of the Salami

## Where it comes from

The Salami is from Norcia in Umbria, a town of butchers so well known for it that the Italian word for a pork butcher is simply the word for someone from Norcia.

Norcia's butchers gave the Italian language the word *norcino*, and the town still trains them. The salami we buy is seasoned with black pepper and wild fennel seed and hung for four months; the supplier sends two a week and will not sell us more, which settles how often this pizza is on the board.

The butcher in Norcia is the third generation of the same family and rebuilt the workshop twice, once after the 2016 earthquake. The limit of two salami a week is not a negotiating position: he cures what the building holds, and he has told Mario twice that he would rather send nothing than send something young.

Norcia sits at nine hundred metres in the Sibillini mountains, which is the whole reason the town became famous for curing: cold, dry winters and almost no humidity let a sausage hang without spoiling. The word *norcino* still means a pork butcher anywhere in Italy, and the trade travelled well enough that the Norcia butchers of Rome had their own guild by the sixteenth century.

## What the creator had in mind

Mario's idea was the opposite of the Pepperoni: a coarse, slowly cured salami cut thick, so that you chew it rather than let it dissolve. He wanted a pizza with a texture, not just a flavour.

Texture is a thing a menu can be designed around, and this pizza is the argument for it: everything else in the house is soft, and one dish that asks to be chewed changes how the meal feels.

## Why these ingredients

Umbria is inland, cool and forested, and its pigs were fattened on acorns. The salami is seasoned with black pepper and fennel and not with paprika, which is what separates it from the American sausage on our Pepperoni.

## How we prepare it

Four millimetres thick, twelve slices, laid in two rings. The salami is added halfway through the bake in our kitchen, which is unusual, and it is why the cook has to stay at the oven.

Twelve slices at four millimetres, laid in two rings, and added through the oven door at second 45 — a step no other pizza here has. Salami on from the start dries into leather; salami on at the halfway point warms through and keeps its fat.

## How we bake it

Ninety seconds at 428 °C, with the oven door opened once at second forty-five. It is the only pizza here that keeps a cook standing at the oven for the whole bake, which is why it is never made in the worst ten minutes of a Saturday.

## What guests ask

**“How is it different from the Pepperoni?”**

Coarser, cut four times thicker, seasoned with pepper and fennel instead of paprika, and not spicy. You chew this one; the other one dissolves.

**“Why is it not always available?”**

Our supplier in Norcia sends two salami a week and will not send more. When they are gone, they are gone.

**“Can I have both salami and pepperoni?”**

Yes, and the kitchen will make them side by side on one pizza so you can taste the difference. It is the only off-menu request Mario actively enjoys.

## How it goes wrong

- salami on before the bake: it dries out and curls into leather
- slices cut thin: the whole point of the pizza disappears
- opening the oven door at the wrong second: the floor loses heat and the base goes pale
- making it during a rush: it needs a cook who can stand still for ninety seconds

## House note

Order this and the Pepperoni together and they will arrive side by side on purpose. Nino and Mario argued about which of the two was the real one for eleven years; the plate settles it better than either of them ever did.
